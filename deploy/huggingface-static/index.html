Catching card testing
before the card gets drained

A near-real-time detector for fraud spikes on a payment stream — card-testing bursts, card-dump enumeration, impossible-travel card use. 24,235 payments/sec on one thread, p99 80 µs, and a stated reason attached to every alert.

The interesting problem isn't finding attacks. It's not flagging the legitimate traffic that looks identical to them — a flash sale, a subscription run, a shop counter, an office network. So the test data contains six such patterns on purpose, and they are scored separately.

Why the evaluation is the hard part

Test a fraud detector on quiet traffic versus obvious attacks and it scores a meaningless 99%, because nothing in the data resembles an attack without being one. Six legitimate patterns were built to break specific rules, and each is scored on its own.

How it works

Each payment becomes ~32 numbers describing the recent behaviour of its card, device, IP and merchant. Everything turns on one constraint: a payment's features may only depend on payments before it.

Two things the work found out about itself

Both were caught by instrumentation that existed to be sceptical of the result, not by a test that happened to fail.

Feature importance, 0.62 vs 0.018 One feature was doing all the work Permutation importance put device_cnt_30s 34× above the next feature. That meant no legitimate device in the data ever ran many cards quickly — the dataset was too easy. Added a POS terminal. Precision fell to 0.767. Fixed the rule with merchant-spread and decline-rate terms → 0.950 on harder data.
20 alerts, 0 reasons Unexplainable alerts were shipping The cost-optimal threshold sat below the floor at which a rule would name itself, so a handful of alerts arrived with no stated cause — defeating the point of a rule-based design. Every alert now names its strongest rule, whatever the threshold.

Three decisions worth defending

The payment's own authorisation result is excluded from its features
Decline ratios come from prior payments only, so the detector works pre-authorisation — at scoring time you don't yet know whether this attempt declines. Enforced by a test: two streams differing only in the final payment's status must produce identical features for it.
The threshold minimises rupees, not F1
A wrongly declined payment costs the merchant its margin plus support and goodwill. A missed fraud costs the full amount plus a ₹1,500 chargeback fee — roughly 50× apart. F1 assumes they're equal, so it was never the objective.
Results are a median over 10 seeds, not a single run
Where attack episodes fall relative to the dev/test cut moves precision several points. The single seed shown in the live console is the weakest of the ten — the demo understates the median rather than flattering it.

Known limits

The data is synthetic. Every number is conditional on the simulator being a fair model of payment traffic. The six hard negatives are the defence against self-congratulation, but real traffic contains look-alikes nobody thought to inject.

All 23 false alarms are individually explained — 11 POS terminals, 7 bystanders at merchants genuinely under attack, 5 victims' own payments alternating with remote fraud. Reassuring about the detector, and a caution about the data: there is no residual pile of unexplained errors because the simulator contains only the confusions put into it.

The gradient-boosted model scores higher (0.980 AP vs 0.796). Rules stay primary because they need no labels — real fraud labels arrive weeks late via chargebacks — and because an analyst can read them. Production would run both.

What it caught, and what it got wrong

per labelled pattern

The table the whole project is built around. Attacks are scored on whether they were detected and how fast; legitimate look-alikes are scored on how often they were wrongly flagged. An aggregate precision figure hides which specific legitimate behaviour a detector cannot tell from fraud — six of these patterns exist purely to make that visible.

Per-payment recall on geo_impossible is only 36% against 100% episode detection: just the payment arriving in the far city shows the velocity jump, and the ones after it look local. One flagged payment is enough to block the card, which is why the episode column is the honest one here — and why the per-payment number is reported rather than quietly dropped.

Priced in rupees

A false positive and a false negative are not interchangeable, and F1 silently assumes they are. Blocking a good payment costs the merchant its margin plus support and goodwill; missing fraud costs the full amount plus a chargeback fee — roughly 50× apart.

Is the threshold real?

The chosen threshold is only as trustworthy as the cost ratio behind it. Re-deriving it under alternative assumptions separates the detector from the accounting.

Where it fails

All 23 false alarms are individually accounted for. That is reassuring about the detector and a caution about the data — there is no residual pile of unexplained errors, because the simulator contains only the confusions deliberately put into it.

  • The data is synthetic. Every number here is conditional on the simulator being a fair model of payment traffic. The six hard negatives are the defence against self-congratulation, but real traffic contains look-alikes nobody thought to inject.
  • Merchant-level rules cause collateral damage by construction. The 7 bystander alerts are arguably correct — during a live attack you would throttle the merchant and accept friction — but they are counted as false positives rather than excused, because the customer whose payment is held does not care why.
  • The gradient-boosted model beats the rules on average precision and recall. The rules stay primary for explainability and because they need no labels — real fraud labels arrive weeks late via chargebacks. A production system would run both.
  • Latency is processing time — feature extraction plus scoring — not end-to-end pipeline time, which would add broker and network hops.
